# Spatial aptitude: derivations

## Coordinate rules for reflections and rotations
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.reflection, ga-spatial.rotation -->

**Claim.** Work in the plane with the origin $O$ and angles measured anticlockwise from the positive $x$-axis. Reflection in the line through $O$ at angle $\varphi$ is

$$S_\varphi(x, y) = (x\cos 2\varphi + y\sin 2\varphi,\; x\sin 2\varphi - y\cos 2\varphi),$$

so the $x$-axis gives $(x, -y)$, the $y$-axis $(-x, y)$, the line $y = x$ gives $(y, x)$ and the line $y = -x$ gives $(-y, -x)$. Rotation about $O$ by $\theta$ anticlockwise is

$$R_\theta(x, y) = (x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta,\; x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta),$$

so $90^\circ$ anticlockwise gives $(-y, x)$, $90^\circ$ clockwise gives $(y, -x)$ and $180^\circ$ gives $(-x, -y)$. Rotations about one centre add their angles, $R_\alpha R_\beta = R_{\alpha+\beta}$, and $R_{360^\circ}$ is the identity, so a chain of turns reduces to one net angle mod $360^\circ$, and $\theta$ clockwise equals $360^\circ - \theta$ anticlockwise. On a clock face the 12 hour marks split $360^\circ$ evenly, $30^\circ$ per mark. "Reflection in the origin", $(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$, is the same map as $R_{180^\circ}$.

**Derivation.** Let $u = (\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$ be a unit vector along the mirror line $\ell$. The foot of the perpendicular from $P = (x, y)$ to $\ell$ is $N = (P\cdot u)\,u$. A reflection moves $P$ along that perpendicular to the same distance on the other side, so $N$ is the midpoint of $P$ and its image $P'$, giving $P' = 2N - P = 2(P\cdot u)\,u - P$. With $P\cdot u = x\cos\varphi + y\sin\varphi$, the first coordinate is $2x\cos^2\varphi + 2y\sin\varphi\cos\varphi - x = x\cos 2\varphi + y\sin 2\varphi$, using $2\cos^2\varphi - 1 = \cos 2\varphi$ and $2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi = \sin 2\varphi$; the second is $2x\sin\varphi\cos\varphi + 2y\sin^2\varphi - y = x\sin 2\varphi - y\cos 2\varphi$, using $2\sin^2\varphi - 1 = -\cos 2\varphi$. Putting $\varphi = 0^\circ, 90^\circ, 45^\circ, 135^\circ$ gives $(\cos 2\varphi, \sin 2\varphi) = (1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 1), (0, -1)$ and the four axis rules. Points of $\ell$ satisfy $P = (P\cdot u)u$, so they stay fixed.

For rotations write $P = (r\cos\alpha, r\sin\alpha)$. Turning by $\theta$ keeps $r$ and adds $\theta$ to the angle, so $P' = (r\cos(\alpha + \theta), r\sin(\alpha + \theta))$, and the angle-addition formulas expand this to $R_\theta$. At $\theta = 90^\circ$, $\cos\theta = 0$ and $\sin\theta = 1$, giving $(-y, x)$; at $\theta = -90^\circ$ the sine is $-1$, giving $(y, -x)$; at $180^\circ$ both coordinates change sign. Composing two rotations adds the angles in the polar form, so $R_\alpha R_\beta = R_{\alpha+\beta}$; a clockwise turn is a negative angle, and $R_{-\theta} = R_{360^\circ - \theta}$ because $R_{360^\circ}$ fixes every point. The point reflection in $O$ sends $P$ to the point $P'$ with $O$ the midpoint of $PP'$, that is $P' = -P$, which is $R_{180^\circ}$; it therefore behaves like a rotation, not like a line reflection (next section).

**GATE use.** "Image of $P$ under a reflection or rotation" is a 1-mark MCQ whose wrong options are the other rules in this list: $(-y, x)$ against $(y, -x)$, and $(y, x)$ against $(-y, -x)$. Test a rule on $(1, 0)$: $90^\circ$ anticlockwise must give $(0, 1)$. Pointer questions add signed turns, reduce mod $360^\circ$ and divide by $30^\circ$.

**Check.** $P(3, -5)$ in $y = -x$ goes to $(5, -3)$; the midpoint $(4, -4)$ lies on $y = -x$, while $(-5, 3)$ is the image in $y = x$ and $(-3, 5)$ is the $180^\circ$ rotation. A pointer at 3 (that is $90^\circ$ clockwise from 12) turned $450^\circ$ anticlockwise and $120^\circ$ clockwise ends at $90 - 450 + 120 = -240 \equiv 120^\circ$ clockwise, the 4 mark; $225^\circ$ from 12 is $225/30 = 7.5$ marks, the 7:30 position. A script applied the general $S_\varphi$ and $R_\theta$ matrices to 1,000 random points and matched every special rule.

## Orientation, and two reflections make a rotation or a translation
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.composition, ga-spatial.reflection, ga-spatial.mirror-water-images -->

**Claim.** Every line reflection reverses orientation (a triangle labelled $ABC$ clockwise becomes anticlockwise) and every rotation and translation preserves it, so no rotation equals a line reflection, and a composite of $r$ line reflections with any rotations and translations reverses orientation exactly when $r$ is odd. Reflecting in a line $\ell$ and then in a line $m$ gives the rotation by $2\alpha$ about $\ell \cap m$, from $\ell$ towards $m$, when the lines meet at angle $\alpha$, and the translation by $2d$ perpendicular to them, from $\ell$ towards $m$, when they are parallel at distance $d$. Rotation and scaling about one centre commute; a nonzero translation and a rotation by an angle that is not a multiple of $360^\circ$ do not.

**Proof.** Every map here has the form $P \mapsto MP + t$ with a $2 \times 2$ matrix $M$. The signed area of triangle $ABC$ is $\tfrac12 \det[B - A,\; C - A]$, positive when $ABC$ runs anticlockwise. Differences of points transform by $M$ alone, since $t$ cancels, and $\det[MX, MY] = \det M \cdot \det[X, Y]$, so the signed area is multiplied by $\det M$. For $S_\varphi$ from the previous section $\det = -\cos^2 2\varphi - \sin^2 2\varphi = -1$, and for $R_\theta$ $\det = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$; a translation has $M = I$. So reflections flip the sign of the signed area and rotations and translations keep it. Determinants multiply under composition, so $r$ reflections contribute $(-1)^r$.

For two lines through $O$, let $J = S_0$ be the reflection in the $x$-axis. Then $S_\varphi = R_{2\varphi}J$, as multiplying the matrices shows, and $J R_\beta J = R_{-\beta}$, because conjugating by a reflection reverses the sense of turning. Hence

$$S_{\varphi_2} S_{\varphi_1} = R_{2\varphi_2} J R_{2\varphi_1} J = R_{2\varphi_2} R_{-2\varphi_1} = R_{2(\varphi_2 - \varphi_1)},$$

a rotation by twice the angle from $\ell$ (at $\varphi_1$) to $m$ (at $\varphi_2$). Lines meeting at another point reduce to this case by moving that point to $O$. For parallel lines take $\ell: x = 0$ and $m: x = d$: the first sends $x \to -x$, the second sends $x \to 2d - x$, so the composite sends $x \to 2d + x$ and leaves $y$ alone, a translation by $2d$.

Scaling about $c$ is $P \mapsto c + k(P - c)$ and rotation about $c$ is $P \mapsto c + R(P - c)$; both act on $P - c$ by a matrix, and $kI$ commutes with every matrix, so the two orders agree. For a translation $T_t$ and rotation $R$ about $O$, $R(P + t) = RP + Rt$ while $T_t R(P) = RP + t$; they agree only if $Rt = t$, which forces $t = 0$ or $R = I$.

**GATE use.** "Which single transformation equals this composite?" Count reflections first: an odd count rules out every rotation option at once. Then compose the coordinate rules in the stated order. A mirror image followed by a water image is reflection in two perpendicular lines, so it is a $180^\circ$ rotation, not a reflection. The trap is applying the steps in the wrong order, which matters as soon as a translation or two non-perpendicular reflections are involved.

**Check.** Rotating $90^\circ$ clockwise and then reflecting in $y = x$: $(x, y) \to (y, -x) \to (-x, y)$, the reflection in the $y$-axis; on $(2, 5)$ this is $(5, -2)$ and then $(-2, 5)$. In matrices, $S_{45^\circ} R_{-90^\circ} = R_{90^\circ} J R_{-90^\circ} = R_{180^\circ} J = S_{90^\circ}$. Reflecting in the $x$-axis and then the $y$-axis (lines at $90^\circ$) gives $R_{180^\circ}$. A script checked $S_{\varphi_2}S_{\varphi_1} = R_{2(\varphi_2 - \varphi_1)}$, the parallel-line translation, both determinants and the non-commuting translation on 1,000 random cases.

## Translation and scaling: lengths, areas and volumes
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.translation-scaling -->

**Claim.** A translation $P \mapsto P + (a, b)$ preserves distances, angles, area and orientation. Scaling by $k \ne 0$ about a centre $c$, $P \mapsto c + k(P - c)$, multiplies every length and perimeter by $\lvert k\rvert$, keeps every angle, multiplies area by $k^2$ and, in space, volume by $\lvert k\rvert^3$; the image is similar to the original. The axis scaling $(x, y) \mapsto (ax, by)$ with $a, b > 0$ multiplies area by $ab$, sends a circle of radius $r$ to an ellipse with semi-axes $ar$ and $br$, and is not a similarity when $a \ne b$. For $k \ne 1$ the centre is the only fixed point, scaling by $k = -1$ about $c$ is the point reflection in $c$, and scalings by $k_1$ then $k_2$ about the same centre make one scaling by $k_1 k_2$.

**Derivation.** For a translation, $(P + t) - (Q + t) = P - Q$, so every distance is unchanged; a triangle keeps its three sides and so, by the law of cosines, its angles and its area. Its matrix is $I$, so orientation is kept (previous section). For scaling, $P' - Q' = k(P - Q)$, so every distance is multiplied by $\lvert k\rvert$. A triangle with sides multiplied by the same factor keeps its angles, since the law of cosines $\cos C = (a^2 + b^2 - c^2)/(2ab)$ is unchanged when all sides scale together. Area: a linear map with matrix $M$ multiplies the area of every parallelogram by $\lvert\det M\rvert$ (the signed area of the previous section), and every region is approximated from inside and outside by unions of small grid squares, so it multiplies every area by $\lvert\det M\rvert$. Here $M = kI$, with $\det = k^2$ in the plane and $k^3$ in space. The centre $c$ only adds a translation, which changes no length or area. For $(ax, by)$, $\det = ab$. A point of the circle $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ goes to $(X, Y) = (ax, by)$ with $(X/a)^2 + (Y/b)^2 = r^2$, an ellipse of area $\pi(ar)(br) = ab \cdot \pi r^2$. When $a \ne b$ the right angle between $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ becomes the angle between $(a, b)$ and $(a, -b)$, whose dot product $a^2 - b^2$ is not zero, so angles change. A fixed point solves $c + k(P - c) = P$, that is $(k - 1)(P - c) = 0$, so $P = c$ when $k \ne 1$. For $k = -1$ the image is $P' = 2c - P$, so $c$ is the midpoint of $PP'$, the point reflection in $c$. Composing, $c + k_2\big(c + k_1(P - c) - c\big) = c + k_1 k_2 (P - c)$.

**GATE use.** "A figure is enlarged by 3 and translated; what is the area of the image?" The answer is $9$ times the area, whatever the translation and centre. The traps are multiplying area by $k$ instead of $k^2$ and letting the translation change the answer.

**Check.** The triangle $(1, 1), (4, 2), (2, 5)$ has area 5.5 by the shoelace formula; scaled by 3 about $(1, -2)$ it has area 49.5, ratio 9. Under $(2x, 5y)$ the area becomes 55, ratio $10 = 2 \cdot 5$. A unit cube scaled by 2 has volume 8. Scaling $(4, 2)$ about $(1, -2)$ by 2 and then by 3 lands where scaling by 6 does, and by $-1$ sends it to $(-2, -6)$, with midpoint $(1, -2)$. The script computed all three areas and both compositions in exact fractions.

## Symmetry of a regular polygon, and how lines and rotations relate
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.symmetry, ga-spatial.composition -->

**Claim.** A regular $n$-gon has exactly $n$ rotational symmetries (by $360^\circ k/n$ for $k = 0, \dots, n-1$), so its order is $n$ and its smallest positive angle is $360^\circ/n$, and exactly $n$ lines of symmetry: for odd $n$ each passes through a vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side; for even $n$, $n/2$ pass through opposite vertices and $n/2$ through midpoints of opposite sides. More generally, if a bounded figure has $m \ge 1$ lines of symmetry and finitely many symmetries, its rotational order is exactly $m$. A figure can have rotational order 2 or more with no line at all (S, Z, N, a general parallelogram), but two or more lines force rotational symmetry.

**Proof.** Every symmetry of a bounded figure fixes its centroid $O$, since isometries carry centroids to centroids, so every rotation symmetry is about $O$ and every mirror line passes through $O$. The rotation symmetries form a finite set closed under composition; let $\theta$ be the smallest positive angle among them. Any rotation symmetry by $\beta$ with $0 \le \beta < 360^\circ$ satisfies $\beta = q\theta + \rho$ with $0 \le \rho < \theta$, and $R_\rho = R_\beta R_\theta^{-q}$ is also a symmetry, so $\rho = 0$. Hence the rotations are the multiples of $\theta$, and since $R_{360^\circ}$ is one of them, $\theta = 360^\circ/r$ for the order $r$.

Fix one mirror line $\ell$ with reflection $S_\ell$. For any rotation symmetry $\rho$, $S_\ell\rho$ is a symmetry that reverses orientation, and an orientation-reversing isometry fixing $O$ is a reflection in a line through $O$: it preserves distances from $O$ and between points, hence dot products, so it is linear with orthonormal columns; the first column is $(\cos a, \sin a)$ and the second is a unit vector perpendicular to it, $\pm(-\sin a, \cos a)$; the minus sign gives $\det = -1$ (previous section) and the matrix $S_{a/2}$. Conversely, for any mirror line $m$, $S_\ell S_m$ is a rotation symmetry and $S_m = S_\ell(S_\ell S_m)$, because $S_\ell S_\ell$ is the identity. So $\rho \mapsto S_\ell\rho$ is a bijection from rotation symmetries to reflection symmetries, and $m = r$.

For the regular $n$-gon with vertices $v_k$ at angles $360^\circ k/n$ about $O$, $R_{360^\circ/n}$ sends $v_k$ to $v_{k+1}$, so all $n$ multiples are symmetries. Any symmetry maps vertices (the points of the polygon farthest from $O$) to vertices and adjacent ones to adjacent ones, and an isometry is fixed by the images of the three non-collinear points $O, v_0, v_1$; $v_0$ has $n$ possible images and then $v_1$ has 2, so there are at most $2n$ symmetries, hence at most $n$ rotations, which makes the order exactly $n$. The line through $O$ and $v_0$ sends $v_k$ to $v_{-k}$, so it is a mirror line, and the bijection gives exactly $n$ lines. Those lines sit at angles $180^\circ k/n$: even $k$ gives a line through a vertex, odd $k$ a line through the midpoint of a side. For odd $n$ the far end of a vertex line is a side midpoint; for even $n$ vertex lines join opposite vertices and midpoint lines join opposite sides.

**GATE use.** MSQs list figures and ask which have line symmetry, rotational symmetry, or both. Test each separately: S and a parallelogram have order 2 with no line; A, T and an isosceles triangle have one line and order 1; H and a rectangle have two lines and so order 2; a square has 4 and 4. The trap is inferring a line of symmetry from rotational symmetry.

**Check.** An equilateral triangle has 3 lines and order 3; a regular hexagon has 6 and 6 with smallest angle $60^\circ$. A script rotated and reflected the vertex set of every regular $n$-gon for $n = 3, \dots, 12$, testing $n$ rotations, one non-symmetric angle and $4n$ candidate mirror angles, and found exactly $n$ rotations and $n$ lines each time.

## Mirror images and water images of letters and words
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.mirror-water-images, ga-spatial.symmetry -->

**Claim.** Write a word of $n$ capital letters $w_1 w_2 \dots w_n$ left to right in equal slots. A mirror standing to the side (reflection in a vertical line) shows $\mu(w_n)\dots\mu(w_1)$, where $\mu$ flips a letter left to right: the order reverses and each letter is flipped. A water image (reflection in a horizontal line below the word) shows $\eta(w_1)\dots\eta(w_n)$, where $\eta$ flips a letter top to bottom: the order stays. Hence, in a font where no capital is the mirror image of a different capital, a word looks unchanged in a mirror exactly when it is a palindrome whose letters all have a vertical line of symmetry (A H I M O T U V W X Y), and unchanged in water exactly when every letter has a horizontal line of symmetry (B C D E H I K O X), with no condition on order. Applying both images gives the word turned through $180^\circ$.

**Proof.** Put slot $i$ at $[i - 1, i]$ on the $x$-axis and the mirror on the line $x = n/2$. Reflection in it is $x \mapsto n - x$, which sends $[i - 1, i]$ to $[n - i, n - i + 1]$, slot $n + 1 - i$, and within the slot reverses left and right. So slot $i$ of the image holds $\mu(w_{n+1-i})$. The image equals the word exactly when $\mu(w_{n+1-i}) = w_i$ for every $i$. By the font assumption $\mu$ of a capital is a capital only when it is the same letter, which happens exactly for letters with a vertical line of symmetry; then the condition becomes $w_{n+1-i} = w_i$ with every letter in that set. The water reflection $y \mapsto -y$ moves no point horizontally, so each slot keeps its letter and only $\eta$ acts; the word is unchanged exactly when $\eta(w_i) = w_i$ for each $i$, that is, each letter has a horizontal line of symmetry. The two mirror lines are perpendicular, so by the composition rule they make a $180^\circ$ rotation.

**GATE use.** "Which option is the mirror (water) image of the word?" For a mirror reverse the order and flip each letter; for water keep the order and flip each letter. The traps are reversing the order for a water image, and thinking a letter with a horizontal line of symmetry survives a mirror (B, C, D, E and K all flip in a mirror). The letter sets depend on the font; in a serif font fewer letters qualify.

**Check.** WATER in a mirror reads R-E-T-A-W with R and E flipped and W, A, T unchanged, since those three have vertical lines. OTTO, WOW, MOM, TOOT and AHA are unchanged in a mirror; NOON is a palindrome but N has no vertical line, so it changes. HIDE, CODE and BOX are unchanged in water; WATER is not, because W, A, T and R lack a horizontal line. Letters in both sets: H, I, O, X. A script applied both tests to these words.

## Mirror image of a clock: actual time is 11:60 minus the time shown
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.clock-images, ga-spatial.mirror-water-images -->

**Claim.** Measure hand angles clockwise from 12. At time $H{:}M$ ($0 \le H \le 11$, $0 \le M < 60$) the minute hand is at $6M$ degrees and the hour hand at $30H + M/2$. A mirror standing beside the clock reflects in the vertical 12–6 line, sending each hand at $\theta$ to $360^\circ - \theta$. The reflected face is always a valid clock showing $11{:}60 - H{:}M$, the same as $12{:}00 - H{:}M$ read on a 12-hour dial, and this rule is its own inverse. The only times that look the same in a mirror are 12:00 and 6:00. A water image, reflection in the horizontal 9–3 line, sends $\theta$ to $180^\circ - \theta$ and never shows a valid time.

**Derivation.** A pair of hand angles $(h, m)$ with $0 \le m < 360$ is a valid face exactly when $h - m/12$ is a multiple of $30^\circ$, because the hour hand sits $M/2 = m/12$ degrees past an hour mark. Under the mirror, for $M > 0$, the minute hand goes to $360 - 6M = 6(60 - M)$, a valid minute reading of $60 - M$, and the hour hand goes to $360 - 30H - M/2 = 30(11 - H) + (60 - M)/2$, exactly the hour hand of $(11 - H){:}(60 - M)$. For $M = 0$ the hands go to $0$ and $30(12 - H)$, the face of $(12 - H){:}00$. Both cases are $11{:}60 - H{:}M$, that is $720 - t$ minutes for $t = 60H + M$, reduced mod 720. Applying the map twice gives $720 - (720 - t) = t$, so the same subtraction converts shown to actual and actual to shown. A time is unchanged when $t \equiv 720 - t$, that is $2t \equiv 0 \pmod{720}$, so $t = 0$ or $t = 360$: 12:00 and 6:00, where both hands lie on the 12–6 line.

For water, the new hands are $h' = 180 - h$ and $m' = 180 - m$, so $h' - m'/12 = 180 - h - 15 + m/12 = 165 - (h - m/12) = 165 - 30H$. Reducing $m'$ into $[0, 360)$ changes $m'/12$ by a multiple of $30$, so $h' - m'/12 \equiv 15^\circ \pmod{30^\circ}$, never a multiple of 30. The hour hand is always half-way between where the minute hand says it should be, so no clock time matches.

**GATE use.** "A clock seen in a mirror shows 4:20; what is the time?" Subtract from 11:60. The traps are subtracting from 12:60 or 12:00 with a borrow slip, and applying the rule to a water image, for which it does not hold.

**Check.** Mirror 4:20 gives $11{:}60 - 4{:}20 = 7{:}40$. At 7:40 the hands are at $230^\circ$ and $240^\circ$; their images $130^\circ$ and $120^\circ$ are the hands of 4:20. Mirror 3:00 gives 9:00. A script checked all 720 minutes of the dial in exact fractions: every mirror image was valid and equal to $720 - t$, the fixed times were 12:00 and 6:00, and none of 43,200 one-second instants gave a valid water image.

## Painted cubes and cuboids, and the number of cuts
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.cube-painting -->

**Claim.** Paint all six faces of an $a \times b \times c$ block with $a, b, c \ge 2$ and cut it into $abc$ unit cubes. The numbers $N_k$ of unit cubes with exactly $k$ painted faces are

$$N_3 = 8,\quad N_2 = 4[(a-2)+(b-2)+(c-2)],\quad N_1 = 2[(a-2)(b-2)+(b-2)(c-2)+(c-2)(a-2)],\quad N_0 = (a-2)(b-2)(c-2),$$

and they add to $abc$. For a cube, $a = b = c = n \ge 2$: $8$, $12(n-2)$, $6(n-2)^2$, $(n-2)^3$, adding to $n^3$. For any set of painted faces, $N_k$ is the coefficient of $t^k$ in the product of one factor per axis, where an axis of length $L \ge 2$ contributes $(L - 2) + e_0 + e_1$ with $e = t$ for a painted end face and $e = 1$ for an unpainted one. Cutting with $p$, $q$ and $r$ full planar cuts parallel to the three pairs of faces gives $(p+1)(q+1)(r+1)$ pieces, and cutting a cube into $n^3$ unit cubes without restacking needs exactly $3(n - 1)$ cuts.

**Derivation.** Give each unit cube coordinates $(x, y, z)$ with $1 \le x \le a$, $1 \le y \le b$, $1 \le z \le c$. A unit cube has a painted face on the side $x = 0$ exactly when $x = 1$, and on the side $x = a$ exactly when $x = a$. When $a \ge 2$ these cannot both hold, so the $x$-coordinate contributes 1 painted face in 2 ways ($x = 1$ or $x = a$) and 0 in $a - 2$ ways; the same holds for $y$ and $z$. The number of painted faces is the number of coordinates that sit at an end. Choosing which coordinates are at an end and multiplying the choices: all three gives $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 8$; two, with the third interior, gives $4(c-2) + 4(a-2) + 4(b-2)$; one gives $2(b-2)(c-2) + 2(a-2)(c-2) + 2(a-2)(b-2)$; none gives the product of the interiors. The total is

$$\big((a-2)+2\big)\big((b-2)+2\big)\big((c-2)+2\big) = abc,$$

and expanding the left side term by term reproduces the four counts, which is why the sum check works. The generating product is the same count with the ends weighted by $t$ when painted and by $1$ when not: multiplying the three factors adds up, over all unit cubes, $t$ raised to the number of painted faces. When $n = 1$ both ends of every axis are the same cube, so the single cube has 6 painted faces and the formulas fail.

For cuts, $p$ distinct planes parallel to one face pair split the edge along that axis into $p + 1$ intervals, and every piece is a product of one interval per axis, so there are $(p+1)(q+1)(r+1)$ pieces. To get unit cubes, every one of the $n - 1$ interior grid planes on each axis must be cut, since the cubes on its two sides end up separate and only a cut in that plane separates them along a whole face; one planar cut through the unrestacked block lies in one plane, so $3(n-1)$ cuts are needed, and these suffice. For a fixed total of cuts, the product is largest when $p, q, r$ are as equal as possible.

**GATE use.** The 1-mark NAT "how many unit cubes have exactly one (two, no) painted faces?" The traps are writing $n$ for $n - 2$, and reusing the all-faces formulas when a face was left unpainted; use the generating product then.

**Check.** $n = 4$: $8 + 24 + 24 + 8 = 64$; $n = 5$: $8 + 36 + 54 + 27 = 125$, so 54 cubes have one painted face. The $4 \times 5 \times 6$ cuboid gives $8, 36, 52, 24$, sum 120. A $4 \times 4 \times 4$ cube painted on all faces but the bottom has the product $(2 + 2t)^2(3 + t) = 12 + 28t + 20t^2 + 4t^3$: 4 cubes with three faces, 20 with two, 28 with one, 12 with none. Nine cuts give at most $4 \cdot 4 \cdot 4 = 64$ pieces. A script classified every unit cube for all cubes up to $n = 8$ and all cuboids with sides 2 to 7, and computed the partial painting by brute force.

## Euler's formula and counting edges from faces
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.polyhedra-euler -->

**Claim.** A convex polyhedron with $V$ vertices, $E$ edges and $F$ faces satisfies $V - E + F = 2$. If every edge borders exactly two faces and joins two vertices, then $2E$ equals the total number of sides of all faces and also the sum of the vertex degrees. Consequences: the five Platonic solids have $(V, E, F) = (4, 6, 4)$, $(8, 12, 6)$, $(6, 12, 8)$, $(20, 30, 12)$, $(12, 30, 20)$; a prism on an $n$-gon has $(2n, 3n, n + 2)$ and a pyramid on an $n$-gon has $(n + 1, 2n, n + 1)$.

**Proof.** Remove the interior of one face and stretch the rest of the surface flat through the hole (for a convex polyhedron, project from a point just outside that face). The result is a connected graph drawn in the plane without crossings, with the same $V$ and $E$, and with $F$ regions: $F - 1$ bounded ones from the remaining faces plus the outer region for the removed face. Prove $V - E + F = 2$ for every connected plane graph by induction on $E$. If the graph has no cycle it is a tree, with $E = V - 1$ and a single region, so $V - (V - 1) + 1 = 2$. Otherwise pick an edge $e$ on a cycle. The cycle is a closed curve, so the regions on its two sides are different, and $e$ separates two different regions. Deleting $e$ keeps the graph connected (the rest of the cycle still joins its ends), merges those two regions, and changes $V - E + F$ by $0 - (-1) + (-1) = 0$. By induction the value is 2.

For the counting identity, each edge has two sides, each lying on one face, so summing the number of sides over all faces counts every edge twice; likewise each edge has two ends. For a Platonic solid with $p$-gon faces and $q$ faces at each vertex, $pF = 2E = qV$; substituting $F = 2E/p$ and $V = 2E/q$ into Euler's formula gives

$$\frac1p + \frac1q = \frac12 + \frac1E .$$

With $p, q \ge 3$ the left side exceeds $\tfrac12$ only for $(p, q) = (3,3), (4,3), (3,4), (5,3), (3,5)$, giving $E = 6, 12, 12, 30, 30$ and the five triples. A prism has two $n$-gon ends with $n$ vertices each, $n$ edges on each end and $n$ vertical edges, and $n$ side faces plus 2 ends; a pyramid has $n$ base vertices plus the apex, $n$ base edges plus $n$ slant edges, and $n$ triangles plus the base. Each triple satisfies Euler's formula.

**GATE use.** NAT questions give the face types and ask for $V$ or $E$: count $2E$ from the faces, then use Euler's formula. The trap is forgetting to halve the side total, or applying $V - E + F = 2$ to a solid with a tunnel through it, for which the value is 0.

**Check.** 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons: $2E = 60 + 120 = 180$, $E = 90$, $V = 2 + 90 - 32 = 60$; every vertex has degree 3, and $3 \cdot 60 = 180$ agrees. The cube: $8 - 12 + 6 = 2$, and its vertex degrees add to $24 = 2 \cdot 12$. A script built the cube from its coordinates and checked the Platonic, prism and pyramid triples for $n = 3, \dots, 19$.

## Cube nets: the eleven nets and the opposite-face rules
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.cube-nets, ga-spatial.patterns-3d -->

**Claim.** Fold a net of six edge-joined unit squares into a cube with the printed side outward. Then: (a) in a straight run of three squares, the two end squares become opposite faces, so in any straight run alternate squares are opposite; (b) in a zigzag of four squares (A–B in one direction, B–C at right angles, C–D in the first direction again), A and D are opposite; (c) two squares that share an edge, or only a corner point, are never opposite; (d) a net never contains a $2 \times 2$ block or five squares in a row. Folding is the same as rolling the cube across the net: the face of the cube resting on each square when the cube is rolled onto it is the face that square becomes. Of the 35 shapes of six squares, exactly 11 are nets.

**Proof.** (a) Lay the middle square B flat as the bottom. A and C are attached across opposite edges of B, and each folds up through $90^\circ$ about its edge, so they stand on opposite sides of B in parallel planes, which are opposite faces. (b) Put C at $[0,1]^2$ as the bottom with B south of it and D east of it. B folds up about $y = 0$ into the south wall, and its west edge becomes the vertical edge $x = 0$, $y = 0$ of that wall. A was attached to B along that edge, so it folds about it into the plane $x = 0$, the west wall. D folds up about $x = 1$ into the east wall, which is opposite. (c) Folding is a continuous map of the sheet onto the cube surface, so a point common to two squares, an edge or a single corner, goes to one point that lies on both faces. Opposite faces of a cube share no point, so the two squares are not opposite, and they are not the same face either. (d) Four squares round one interior point of the net would make that point a cube vertex lying on four faces, but each vertex of a cube lies on exactly three. In a row of five, squares 1 and 3 are opposite and so are 3 and 5, so 1 and 5 would be the same face.

Rolling: with the cube resting on square $s$, folding the neighbouring square $t$ up about their common edge makes $t$ the face across that edge from the bottom; tipping the cube over the same edge brings exactly that face down onto $t$. Repeating along the net assigns a face to every square, and the shape is a net exactly when the six assigned faces are distinct. The screens in (d) are not complete: 12 of the 24 shapes that fail have neither a $2 \times 2$ block nor a row of five, for example a row of four with one square above its first and one above its third square, where squares 1 and 3 become opposite faces by (a) and each extra square folds up from the same-side edge of its square, so both become the same face, the wall on that side.

Cyclic order: three squares meeting at a corner of the net (an L of three) fold into the three faces round one vertex, and seen from outside they keep the cyclic order they have in the net, because folding along the two edges closes the missing $90^\circ$ gap without turning any square over.

**GATE use.** "Which cube can be folded from this net?" Mark the opposite pairs with (a) and (b), strike every option that shows two of them side by side, then check the cyclic order of the three visible faces. The trap is an option whose three faces are pairwise adjacent but appear in the mirror-image order round the corner.

**Check.** The staircase net A(0,2), B(1,2), C(1,1), D(2,1), E(2,0), F(3,0) folds with A opposite D, B opposite E and C opposite F. A script generated all 35 free hexominoes, folded each by rolling a cube across it, found 11 nets, and on every net confirmed (a) on 20 straight triples, (b) on 20 zigzags, and (c) on all 68 corner-touching pairs and every edge-sharing pair.

## Dice: opposite faces from corner views, standard dice and rolling
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.dice, ga-spatial.patterns-3d -->

**Claim.** A die is a cube with the numbers 1 to 6 on its faces; a view shows the three faces at one corner, read clockwise as seen from outside. (a) Faces seen in one view are pairwise adjacent, so never opposite; each face has exactly four neighbours, so once four distinct neighbours of $X$ are known, the sixth number is opposite $X$. (b) If two views share exactly one face $c$ and are read clockwise as $(c, x_1, x_2)$ and $(c, y_1, y_2)$, then $x_1$ is opposite $y_1$, $x_2$ is opposite $y_2$, and the number in neither view is opposite $c$. (c) If two views share two faces $a$ and $b$, their third faces are opposite. (d) On a standard die (opposite faces sum to 7) each view shows one number from each of the pairs 1–6, 2–5, 3–4, and there are two standard dice, mirror images of each other. (e) Rolling a die one step over a bottom edge moves the top face to the side it rolls towards; four rolls in one direction restore the die, and rolls in different directions do not commute.

**Proof.** (a) Place the cube with faces on the planes $x = \pm1$, $y = \pm1$, $z = \pm1$. The vertex $(s_x, s_y, s_z)$ lies on exactly one face of each opposite pair, so the three faces at a corner contain no opposite pair. A face is adjacent to every face except itself and its opposite, which is four faces.

(b) Let the neighbours of $c$, in clockwise order seen from outside $c$, be $n_0, n_1, n_2, n_3$ (indices mod 4); $n_i$ and $n_{i+2}$ meet $c$ along opposite edges, so they are parallel, hence opposite. The corners of $c$ lie between consecutive neighbours. The quarter-turn of the die about the axis through $c$ sends $n_i$ to $n_{i+1}$ and each corner of $c$ to the next one, and as a rotation it preserves clockwise order. So if the corner between $n_0$ and $n_1$ reads $(c, n_0, n_1)$ clockwise, every corner reads $(c, n_i, n_{i+1})$, and if it reads $(c, n_1, n_0)$, every corner reads $(c, n_{i+1}, n_i)$. Two views sharing only $c$ are corners $i$ and $j$ with $\{n_i, n_{i+1}\}$ disjoint from $\{n_j, n_{j+1}\}$, which forces $j = i + 2$. In either reading the matching positions hold $n_i$ and $n_{i+2}$, then $n_{i+1}$ and $n_{i+3}$: opposite pairs. The two views show $c$ and all four of its neighbours, so the missing number is opposite $c$.

(c) Views containing $a$ and $b$ are the two corners at the ends of edge $ab$. With the neighbours of $a$ in order $b, p, q, r$, those corners are between $b, p$ and between $r, b$, so the third faces are $p$ and $r$, two apart in the cycle round $a$, hence opposite. Read clockwise, one view is $(a, b, x)$ and the other is $(b, a, y)$.

(d) By (a) a view meets each opposite pair once, and on a standard die the pairs are 1–6, 2–5, 3–4. Fixing 1 on top and 2 in front, 3 can be on the right or the left, and these two dice are mirror images, which no rotation relates. So a standard die does not fix whether 1, 2, 3 run clockwise round their corner.

(e) A roll is a $90^\circ$ rotation about the bottom edge in the direction of travel: top goes to that side, that side to the bottom, bottom to the opposite side, the opposite side to the top, and the two faces on the axis stay. Four quarter-turns about one axis make $360^\circ$.

**GATE use.** The 1-mark MCQ "which number is opposite 1?" from two or three views. Collect neighbours for (a), or use (b) with one common face; use the sum-7 rule only for a die called standard. The trap is treating two numbers seen together as possible opposites.

**Check.** Views (5, 1, 4) and (5, 2, 6) give 1–2, 4–6 and 3–5, and every numbering consistent with them puts 2 opposite 1. Views (1, 2, 3), (1, 3, 4), (6, 4, 3) give 3 the neighbours 1, 2, 4, 6, so 5 is opposite 3. A standard die with top 1, north 2, east 3 rolled north then east shows 4 on top; east then north shows 5. A script enumerated all 720 numberings with exact clockwise readings and confirmed (a) at every corner, (b) on 8,640 pairs of views with one common face, (c) on 8,640 pairs with two, the two standard dice, and the rolls.

## Folding and punching: $2^k$ holes, and $2^{k-1}$ on the last crease
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.paper-folding-punching -->

**Claim.** Fold a flat sheet $k$ times; fold $j$ reflects the part of the current packet on one side of a line $L_j$ onto the other side, and every fold is a half-fold: the flap is exactly the mirror image of the kept side, so it covers it and the new packet is the kept side. Punch $h$ small holes through the whole packet. A hole that lies on none of the crease lines (at the moment each fold was made) becomes $2^k$ holes in the opened sheet; a hole centred on the crease of the last fold, and on no earlier one, becomes $2^{k-1}$ holes, each straddling that crease; in general a hole lying on the creases of $j$ of the folds becomes $2^{k-j}$ holes. Totals are $h \cdot 2^k$ and $h \cdot 2^{k-1}$, less any copies that overlap. The opened pattern is found by undoing the folds last first, reflecting every hole across each crease in turn. It is symmetric about the first crease line, and each later crease is a mirror for the two panels it joins.

**Proof.** Let $f_j$ be the map of fold $j$: the identity on the kept side of $L_j$ and the reflection in $L_j$ on the other side. A point of the final packet came from the sheet by $F = f_k \circ \dots \circ f_1$, and the paper removed by a punch at $p$ is $F^{-1}$ of the punched region. Under a single $f_j$, a point on the kept side off $L_j$ has two preimages, itself and its mirror image, which is paper because the fold is a half-fold, and a point on $L_j$ has one, itself. Preimages of different points are different, so the counts multiply along the chain: $2^k$ for a point that avoids every crease, and a factor 1 instead of 2 for each fold whose crease it lies on. The first preimage taken is under $f_k$, which is why the last fold is undone first. Without the half-fold assumption the count fails: fold the strip $[0, 4]$ once at $x = 1$, left over right, and punch at $x = 3$; the mirror point $-1$ is off the sheet, so the opened strip has 1 hole, not 2. A fold such as a letter fold into thirds needs the preimages counted directly.

For a hole of positive size, take a disc small enough to stay away from every crease it does not touch. Off the creases, each $f_j^{-1}$ turns one disc into two disjoint discs, giving $2^k$ separate holes. A hole at the folded edge of fold $k$ cuts a half-disc of the packet; its preimage under $f_k$ is that half-disc and its mirror image, which join along $L_k$ into one disc, so the last fold contributes a factor 1, and the $k - 1$ earlier folds double it as before. Physically, the packet edge of fold $k$ consists of $2^{k-1}$ U-bends, one per layer before the fold, and the punch cuts each bend once.

Each $f_j^{-1}$ adds the mirror image of every hole across $L_j$, so after undoing fold $j$ the pattern on the paper present at that stage is symmetric about $L_j$. The first crease spans the whole sheet, so the final pattern is symmetric about it; a later crease spans only the panels it joins, and the symmetry holds there, not across the whole sheet.

**GATE use.** The 2-mark MCQ "which pattern appears when the sheet is opened?" and the NAT "how many holes?" Unfold one crease at a time and reject options that break the symmetry about a crease. The trap is doubling a hole on a crease, which gives one hole across the crease per bend.

**Check.** A $4 \times 4$ square folded along $x = 2$ (left onto right) and then $y = 2$ (bottom onto top), punched at $(3.5, 2.5)$, opens to $(3.5, 2.5), (3.5, 1.5), (0.5, 2.5), (0.5, 1.5)$. Folding left over right, bottom over top, then left over right again along $x = 3$, an interior hole gives 8 holes and a hole centred on the $x = 3$ edge gives 4, total 12; the pattern is symmetric about $x = 2$ and $y = 2$, but about $x = 3$ only within the right half. A hole on both $x = 3$ and the image of $y = 2$ gives $2^{3-2} = 2$ holes. A script computed exact preimages and counted connected components of the preimage of each punched disc.

## Crease counts from repeated folds
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.crease-patterns, ga-spatial.paper-folding-punching -->

**Claim.** Folding a strip in half $k$ times, always in the same direction, and opening it leaves $2^k - 1$ parallel creases that cut it into $2^k$ equal panels; fold $j$ adds $2^{j-1}$ new creases, twice as many as fold $j - 1$. Folding a square along one centre line, then the other, then folding the quarter along its diagonal through the centre leaves 4 crease lines through the centre and 8 congruent triangles. For parallel creases, panels = creases + 1; a letter fold into thirds gives 3 layers, 2 creases and 3 panels. A strip folded in half $k$ times in the same direction and cut once straight across all layers, at a point on no crease, falls into $2^k + 1$ pieces.

**Derivation.** Before fold $j$ the packet has $2^{j-1}$ layers, each fold doubling the layer count. Fold $j$ bends every layer once along the fold line, and each bend is a separate crease in the opened strip, so fold $j$ adds $2^{j-1}$ creases. In total

$$\sum_{j=1}^{k} 2^{j-1} = 2^k - 1 .$$

After $k$ half-folds the packet has length $L/2^k$ for a strip of length $L$, and its $2^k$ layers are the panels of the opened strip, so the panels are equal. A strip cut at $c$ parallel lines has $c + 1$ pieces, matching $2^k = (2^k - 1) + 1$. For the square, the first fold makes the first centre line; the second fold bends 2 layers, making 2 segments that together form the other centre line; the third bends 4 layers along the half-diagonal of the quarter, and undoing the two earlier folds reflects that segment into all four half-diagonals, which form both diagonals. Four lines through one point divide the square into 8 regions, and the reflections carry any one triangle onto all the others, so they are congruent. A letter fold stacks three panels, and the two bends are the two creases.

For the cut, the packet's $2^k$ layers are the $2^k$ panels, and a cut across the packet at distance $t$ from one end, with $0 < t < L/2^k$, cuts each panel once, at the image of $t$ under the reflections that unfold it. These $2^k$ cut points lie in different panels and none is on a crease, so they are distinct, and a strip cut at $2^k$ points falls into $2^k + 1$ pieces. The pieces between two cuts stay joined across the crease that lies between them, which is why the count is not $2 \cdot 2^k$.

**GATE use.** NAT "how many creases (panels) after $k$ folds?" The trap is answering $2k$ or $2^k$ instead of $2^k - 1$, and applying panels = creases + 1 when the creases cross.

**Check.** $k = 4$: $1 + 2 + 4 + 8 = 15$ creases and 16 panels. The square pattern has $1 + 2 + 4 = 7$ crease segments and 8 triangles. A script folded a unit strip $k = 1, \dots, 6$ times in exact fractions and counted $1, 3, 7, 15, 31, 63$ creases. Cutting the packet once off the creases, the same script counted $3, 5, 9, 17, 33, 65$ pieces; $k = 3$ gives 9.

## Squares and rectangles in a grid, and colouring obstructions
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.assembling-grouping -->

**Claim.** An $m \times n$ grid of unit squares contains $\sum_{s \ge 1} (m - s + 1)(n - s + 1)$ squares (over $s \le \min(m, n)$) and $\binom{m+1}{2}\binom{n+1}{2}$ rectangles; for $m = n$ these are

$$1^2 + 2^2 + \dots + n^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \binom{n+1}{2}^2 .$$

Every domino placed on a chessboard-coloured board covers one dark and one light square, so a region with unequal colour counts cannot be tiled by dominoes; an $8 \times 8$ board with two opposite corners removed has 30 squares of one colour and 32 of the other and cannot be tiled by 31 dominoes.

**Proof.** A rectangle in the grid is fixed by its left and right sides, two of the $m + 1$ vertical grid lines, and its top and bottom, two of the $n + 1$ horizontal lines; every such choice gives one rectangle, so the count is $\binom{m+1}{2}\binom{n+1}{2}$. An $s \times s$ square is fixed by its top-left corner, which can sit in $m - s + 1$ columns and $n - s + 1$ rows. For $m = n$ the sum is $\sum_{s=1}^{n} (n - s + 1)^2 = \sum_{j=1}^{n} j^2 = S$. To evaluate $S$, sum $(j+1)^3 - j^3 = 3j^2 + 3j + 1$ over $j = 1, \dots, n$; the left side telescopes to $(n+1)^3 - 1$, so

$$(n+1)^3 - 1 = 3S + \frac{3n(n+1)}{2} + n, \qquad S = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$$

after factoring $(n + 1)$ out of $(n+1)^3 - 1 - n - \tfrac32 n(n+1) = (n+1)\big(n^2 + \tfrac n2\big)$.

Colour square $(i, j)$ by the parity of $i + j$. A domino covers two squares that differ by 1 in one coordinate, so their parities differ: one of each colour. A tiling by $d$ dominoes therefore covers $d$ squares of each colour. The opposite corners $(1, 1)$ and $(8, 8)$ both have even $i + j$, so removing them leaves 30 even and 32 odd squares, and 31 dominoes would need 31 of each. The colour test is necessary, not sufficient, so a balanced region still needs a construction. For assembly in general, total area and any such colour counts must match, and a flat piece may be rotated but turned over only when the question allows it: by the orientation result, an L piece and its mirror image are not related by any rotation.

**GATE use.** "How many squares (rectangles) in the figure?" for a full grid; "can these pieces form the shape?" for the colouring argument. The traps are counting only unit squares, using $n^2$ for the rectangles, and accepting a tiling because the area is even.

**Check.** $4 \times 4$: 30 squares and 100 rectangles; $8 \times 8$: 204 and 1296; $3 \times 5$: 26 and 90. A script counted squares and rectangles by brute force on every square grid up to $8 \times 8$ and every grid up to $6 \times 6$, found 36 and 6,728 domino tilings of the full $4 \times 4$ and $6 \times 6$ boards and none with opposite corners removed, and counted 30 and 32 colours on the $8 \times 8$ board.

## Views of cube stacks and plane sections of a cube
<!-- topics: ga-spatial.patterns-3d -->

**Claim.** Stack unit cubes on an $R \times C$ grid with $h(i, j) \ge 0$ cubes in cell $(i, j)$. The front view gives the column heights $F_j = \max_i h(i, j)$ and the side view the row heights $S_i = \max_j h(i, j)$; the top view is the set $T$ of occupied cells. Given views that come from some stack, the largest number of cubes is $\sum_{(i,j) \in T} \min(S_i, F_j)$. With only the front and side views given, the smallest number is $\sum_{v \ge 1} v \cdot \max(c_v, r_v)$, where $c_v$ and $r_v$ are the numbers of columns and rows of height $v$. A plane meeting a cube in a polygon gives a polygon with 3 to 6 sides; a pentagon section is never regular, and the plane through the midpoints of six edges, perpendicular to a body diagonal, gives a regular hexagon.

**Proof.** Maximum: every cell satisfies $h(i, j) \le F_j$ and $h(i, j) \le S_i$, and is 0 outside $T$, so the total is at most the sum of $\min(S_i, F_j)$ over $T$. Setting $h = \min(S_i, F_j)$ on $T$ keeps each column at most $F_j$ and each row at most $S_i$. In the original stack some cell $(i, j)$ of column $j$ has height $F_j$, so $S_i \ge F_j$ and the new height there is exactly $F_j$; the rows work the same way, so the views are unchanged and the bound is attained.

Minimum: each column of height $v$ needs a cell of height exactly $v$, and so does each row of height $v$. One cell of height $v$ serves at most one column and one row, so at least $\max(c_v, r_v)$ cells have height $v$, cells serving different heights are different, and the total is at least $\sum_v v \max(c_v, r_v)$. For the construction, pair the columns and rows of height $v$; an unpaired column of height $v$ is paired with a row of the greatest height, which is at least $v$ because the tallest column and tallest row have the same height, and an unpaired row likewise. Put $v$ cubes at each paired cell and none elsewhere: no cell exceeds its row or column height, and every height is reached. When a top view is also given, the stack must use every cell of $T$, so the minimum can only rise.

Sections: the cube and the plane are convex, so their intersection is a convex polygon. Each side of it lies in one face, since a face is a convex square meeting the plane in at most one segment, so there are at most 6 sides, and a polygon has at least 3. A pentagon uses 5 faces, so it uses both faces of at least two opposite pairs, and the two sides in parallel faces are parallel lines; a regular pentagon has no parallel sides. In $[0,1]^3$ the plane $x + y + z = 3/2$ has normal $(1, 1, 1)$, the body diagonal. An edge has two coordinates fixed at 0 or 1; the plane meets it exactly when those two sum to 1, and then at the free coordinate $\tfrac12$, its midpoint. That happens on 6 edges, and consecutive midpoints such as $(1, \tfrac12, 0)$ and $(1, 0, \tfrac12)$ are $\sqrt2/2$ apart.

**GATE use.** "Given the front and side views, what is the least (greatest) number of cubes?" Build the min-of-heights grid for the maximum and pair equal heights for the minimum. The trap is assuming the views fix the count. Section questions ask which polygons can occur; a regular pentagon and any polygon with more than 6 sides are the wrong options.

**Check.** Front $(2, 3, 1)$ and side $(3, 2, 1)$ on a $3 \times 3$ grid: maximum 14 with all cells occupied, minimum $1 + 2 + 3 = 6$. The hexagon's six vertices are midpoints, and its pairwise distances take only the values $\sqrt2/2$, $\sqrt6/2$ and $\sqrt2$, the side, short diagonal and long diagonal of a regular hexagon of side $\sqrt2/2$. A script enumerated every stack of height up to 3 on $2 \times 2$, $2 \times 3$ and $3 \times 3$ grids, 2,240 view classes, and matched both formulas, the maximum for every top view.
