# Stacks, queues and linked lists: derivations

## Row-major, column-major and triangular addresses
<!-- topics: pds-linear-data-structures.array-address, pds-linear-data-structures.arrays -->

**Claim.** Let $A[l_1..u_1][l_2..u_2]$ be stored contiguously, without gaps, from base address $B$, with $w$ bytes per element, $m = u_1 - l_1 + 1$ rows and $n = u_2 - l_2 + 1$ columns. Then

$$\mathrm{Addr}_{\text{row}}(A[i][j]) = B + \big((i - l_1)\,n + (j - l_2)\big)\,w, \qquad \mathrm{Addr}_{\text{col}}(A[i][j]) = B + \big((j - l_2)\,m + (i - l_1)\big)\,w.$$

For three dimensions $A[l_1..u_1][l_2..u_2][l_3..u_3]$ in row-major order, with $n_2$ and $n_3$ the sizes of the last two dimensions,

$$\mathrm{Addr}(A[i][j][k]) = B + \Big(\big((i - l_1)\,n_2 + (j - l_2)\big)\,n_3 + (k - l_3)\Big)\,w .$$

A lower-triangular $n \times n$ matrix, 1-indexed and stored row by row with only the entries $j \le i$, holds $n(n+1)/2$ elements, and $a_{ij}$ lies at $B + \left(\frac{i(i-1)}{2} + j - 1\right)w$; with 0-indexing the offset is $\frac{i(i+1)}{2} + j$.

**Derivation.** In a gap-free layout an element's address is $B$ plus $w$ times the number of elements stored before it, so every formula is a count. In row-major order the elements before $A[i][j]$ are all of rows $l_1, \dots, i-1$, which is $i - l_1$ full rows of $n$ elements, and then the entries of row $i$ in columns $l_2, \dots, j-1$, which is $j - l_2$ more. Column-major order stores whole columns first, so $j - l_2$ full columns of $m$ elements precede the column of $A[i][j]$, and $i - l_1$ entries of that column precede the element. The lower bounds enter only through these subtractions: a row index $i$ is the $(i - l_1)$-th row counted from zero. For three dimensions, apply the two-dimensional count twice: $(i - l_1)$ full planes of $n_2 n_3$ elements, then $(j - l_2)$ full rows of $n_3$, then $(k - l_3)$; factoring $n_3$ gives the nested form. In the triangle, row $r$ stores the $r$ entries $a_{r1}, \dots, a_{rr}$, so rows $1$ to $i-1$ hold $1 + 2 + \dots + (i-1) = i(i-1)/2$ entries, and $a_{ij}$ is preceded by $j - 1$ entries of its own row. Setting $i = n$, $j = n + 1$ in the count gives the total $n(n+1)/2$. Shifting to 0-indexing replaces $i$ by $i + 1$ and $j - 1$ by $j$.

**GATE use.** A 1-mark NAT gives bounds, a base and an element size. The traps are ignoring a negative lower bound (it must be subtracted, so $-10$ adds 10 rows), multiplying by the number of columns in column-major order instead of the number of rows, and forgetting to multiply the element count by $w$. Variants on triangular storage follow from the same count: column-major lower triangle gives offset $(j-1)n - \frac{(j-1)(j-2)}{2} + (i - j)$, and row-major upper triangle gives $(i-1)n - \frac{(i-1)(i-2)}{2} + (j - i)$.

**Check.** $A[-10..10][5..25]$ with $w = 4$ and $B = 1000$: 21 columns, and $A[0][10]$ has $10 \cdot 21 + 5 = 215$ elements before it in row-major order, address 1860; in column-major order it has $5 \cdot 21 + 10 = 115$, address 1460. For $A[1..3][-2..2][0..3]$ with $B = 500$ and $w = 2$, $A[2][1][2]$ has offset $((1 \cdot 5 + 3) \cdot 4 + 2) = 34$, address 568. In a $5 \times 5$ lower triangle, $a_{42}$ has offset $6 + 1 = 7$ and $a_{55}$ offset 14, the last of 15. A script enumerated every element of 300 random 2-D arrays, 200 random 3-D arrays and triangles up to $n = 11$ in storage order and matched each formula, the four triangular variants included.

## Dynamic arrays by doubling, and sparse triplets
<!-- topics: pds-linear-data-structures.arrays -->

**Claim.** A dynamic array starts with capacity 1. An append into a full array of size $s$ allocates capacity $2s$ and copies the $s$ elements before writing the new one. Over $n \ge 2$ appends the total number of copies is

$$\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor \log_2 (n-1) \rfloor} 2^k = 2^{\lfloor \log_2(n-1) \rfloor + 1} - 1 < 2n,$$

so the total work is $O(n)$ and an append costs $O(1)$ amortized, although one append can cost $\Theta(n)$. (For $n = 1$ there are no copies.) Growing by a fixed $c$ instead, starting from capacity $c$, costs $c\,\frac{q(q+1)}{2}$ copies with $q = \lfloor (n-1)/c \rfloor$, which is about $n^2/(2c)$. A sparse $m \times n$ matrix with $t$ nonzero entries in triplet form, with one header triplet $(m, n, t)$, uses $3(t+1)$ words, fewer than $mn$ exactly when $t < mn/3 - 1$.

**Derivation.** Capacities run through $1, 2, 4, \dots$, and a copy happens on the append that finds size equal to capacity $2^k$, that is on append number $2^k + 1$, and copies $2^k$ elements. Over $n$ appends this happens for every $k$ with $2^k + 1 \le n$, so $k$ runs from 0 to $K = \lfloor \log_2(n-1) \rfloor$. The geometric sum is $2^{K+1} - 1$, and $2^K \le n - 1$ gives $2^{K+1} - 1 \le 2n - 3 < 2n$. Adding the $n$ writes, the total is under $3n$, so the average over the $n$ appends is below 3, a constant. The expensive append is the one with $n - 1$ a power of two, which copies $n - 1$ elements. With growth by $c$, the copies happen at sizes $c, 2c, \dots, qc$ where $qc \le n - 1$, and $c(1 + 2 + \dots + q) = cq(q+1)/2$, which is $\Theta(n^2/c)$: a fixed increment does not give $O(1)$ amortized appends. The doubling factor matters only through being a constant $r > 1$; any such factor gives a geometric sum below $\frac{r}{r-1}n$. For triplets, each nonzero entry costs three words (row, column, value) and the header three more, so $3(t + 1) < mn$ rearranges to $t < mn/3 - 1$. Access to $(i, j)$ is a search over $t$ triplets instead of $O(1)$.

**GATE use.** MCQs ask for the amortized cost of an append (constant) against the worst case of one append ($\Theta(n)$), and for the total cost of $n$ appends with additive growth ($\Theta(n^2)$ for a constant $c$). Sparse-matrix questions ask for the storage count or the break-even density. The trap is reporting the worst case of a single operation as the amortized cost, or the other way round.

**Check.** 9 appends copy $1 + 2 + 4 + 8 = 15$ elements, 16 appends also 15, 17 appends 31, and 1000 appends 1023, each below $2n$. A simulation matched the closed form for every $n$ from 2 to 4999. Growth by 10 over 1000 appends copies 49,500 elements ($q = 99$), close to $n^2/(2c) = 50{,}000$; by 100 over 10,000 appends it copies 495,000. A $6 \times 6$ matrix with 8 nonzeros needs 27 words in triplets against 36; a $100 \times 100$ matrix with 50 nonzeros needs 153 against 10,000.

## Array stacks and two stacks sharing one array
<!-- topics: pds-linear-data-structures.stack, pds-linear-data-structures.two-stacks-one-array -->

**Claim.** An array stack $S[0..N-1]$ with $\mathit{top}$ starting at $-1$ holds $\mathit{top} + 1$ elements, so it is empty when $\mathit{top} = -1$ and full when $\mathit{top} = N - 1$; push is $S[{+}{+}\mathit{top}] = x$ and pop returns $S[\mathit{top}{-}{-}]$, each $O(1)$. Two stacks share $A[0..N-1]$ when stack 1 grows up from $\mathit{top}_1 = -1$ and stack 2 grows down from $\mathit{top}_2 = N$. Then the free cells are exactly the indices $\mathit{top}_1 + 1, \dots, \mathit{top}_2 - 1$, and a push must report overflow exactly when $\mathit{top}_1 + 1 = \mathit{top}_2$, which happens only when all $N$ cells are in use. A linked stack must push and pop at the head.

**Proof.** The invariant for one stack is that $S[0..\mathit{top}]$ holds the elements, bottom first. It holds initially (an empty range at $\mathit{top} = -1$); push increments $\mathit{top}$ and writes the new top, pop reads the top and decrements, and each keeps the invariant. The count $\mathit{top}+1$ is $N$ exactly at $\mathit{top} = N - 1$. For the shared array, the invariant is that stack 1 occupies $A[0..\mathit{top}_1]$ and stack 2 occupies $A[\mathit{top}_2..N-1]$, with $\mathit{top}_1 < \mathit{top}_2$. The two ranges are disjoint, and the cells between them, $\mathit{top}_2 - \mathit{top}_1 - 1$ in number, are free. A push onto stack 1 needs cell $\mathit{top}_1 + 1$ free, which fails only when $\mathit{top}_1 + 1 = \mathit{top}_2$; a push onto stack 2 needs $\mathit{top}_2 - 1$ free, the same condition. When it holds, $(\mathit{top}_1 + 1) + (N - \mathit{top}_2) = N$, so the stacks together hold all $N$ elements. A fixed split into halves gives each stack $N/2$ cells regardless of the other's use, so it can refuse a push while $N/2$ cells stand empty. In a singly linked stack the head is the only end at which both insertion and removal are $O(1)$; popping at the tail would need the node before the tail, an $O(n)$ walk.

**GATE use.** The MCQ on two stacks asks for the overflow test: $\mathit{top}_1 + 1 = \mathit{top}_2$. Distractors test $\mathit{top}_1 = \mathit{top}_2$, which means the stacks already overlap, or a test against $N/2$, which is the wasteful fixed split. In single-stack code the trap is an off-by-one full test, $\mathit{top} = N$ instead of $N - 1$.

**Check.** A script ran 2,000 random sequences of 40 pushes and pops onto the two stacks with $N$ from 1 to 8 and confirmed that $\mathit{top}_1 + 1 = \mathit{top}_2$ held exactly when the two stacks held $N$ elements in total. With $N = 10$ split into halves, a sixth push onto stack 1 overflows while stack 2 is empty; the shared layout accepts ten pushes onto stack 1.

## Stack permutations: the greedy test, the forbidden pattern and the Catalan count
<!-- topics: pds-linear-data-structures.stack-permutations -->

**Claim.** Push $1, 2, \dots, n$ in that order, with pops allowed at any time, until all are popped. For an output sequence $\pi$ the following are equivalent.

1. $\pi$ can be produced.
2. The greedy test succeeds: for each $x$ of $\pi$ in turn, push inputs until $x$ has been pushed, then $x$ must be on top, and pop it.
3. $\pi$ has no three values $x, y, z$ appearing in that order with $y < z < x$ (no 312 pattern).

The number of producible outputs is the Catalan number $C_n = \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}$: 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132 for $n = 1$ to 6.

**Proof.** (2 implies 1) The greedy run is a valid run. (1 implies 2) Take any run producing $\pi$ and compare it with the greedy run. Before output $x$, $x$ must have been pushed, and inputs come in order, so everything up to $x$ has been pushed; pushing beyond $x$ before popping it would put a larger value above $x$, which must leave before $x$, contradicting that $x$ is the next output. So the run's stack before each pop equals the greedy stack, and $x$ is on top in both. The same argument shows that each producible output comes from exactly one word of pushes and pops.

(3 fails implies 1 fails) Suppose $x, y, z$ appear in order with $y < z < x$. When $x$ is popped, $y$ and $z$ have been pushed (both are smaller than $x$) and are still inside (both come out later), and $z$, pushed after $y$, lies above $y$. So $z$ must come out before $y$, a contradiction.

(2 fails implies 3 fails) Suppose the greedy test fails at output $y$: $y$ has been pushed but is not on top, and the top is some $z$ pushed after $y$, so $z > y$ and $z$ comes later in $\pi$. Inputs are pushed only to reach an output, so the largest value pushed so far, $x$, was itself an earlier output; $x \ge z$, and $x \ne z$ because $z$ is still inside. Then $x, y, z$ appear in that order with $y < z < x$.

For the count, the bijection with runs means counting words of $n$ pushes and $n$ pops in which no prefix has more pops than pushes. Of all $\binom{2n}{n}$ words, a bad one has a first prefix with one more pop than push; flipping every symbol after that prefix gives a word with $n + 1$ pops and $n - 1$ pushes, and the map is invertible (flip after the first point where pops lead), so there are $\binom{2n}{n-1}$ bad words. Then $\binom{2n}{n} - \binom{2n}{n-1} = \binom{2n}{n}\left(1 - \frac{n}{n+1}\right) = \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}$. This is the reflection argument for ballot sequences, as in Knuth's treatment of stack permutations.

**GATE use.** The 1-mark MCQ lists four orders and asks which cannot be produced: run the greedy test on each option, or scan for a large value followed by a smaller one followed by one in between. The NAT asks for the number of orders, $C_n$. The trap is reading a pattern loosely, for instance thinking that any descent after a peak is fatal: 3, 4, 2, 1 is valid.

**Check.** 3, 4, 2, 1 and 2, 1, 4, 3 and 4, 3, 2, 1 pass the greedy test; 3, 1, 2, 4 fails (2 sits on 1 after 3 leaves), as do 4, 1, 3, 2 and 1, 4, 2, 3. For $n = 1$ to 8 a script enumerated every permutation, compared the greedy test, the 312-pattern test and an exhaustive search over push/pop runs, found all three agree, and counted 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132, 429, 1430, equal to $\binom{2n}{n}/(n+1)$. It also confirmed that each output comes from exactly one push/pop word for $n \le 7$, and that the number of valid words is $C_n$ for $n \le 6$.

## Restricted deques and the monotonic deque
<!-- topics: pds-linear-data-structures.deque-priority-queue -->

**Claim.** Feed $1, 2, \dots, n$ in order into a deque. An input-restricted deque inserts only at the rear and deletes at both ends; an output-restricted deque inserts at both ends and deletes only at the front. Both produce every stack output and more: for $n = 4$ each produces 22 of the 24 orders against the stack's 14. The input-restricted deque cannot produce 4, 2, 1, 3 or 4, 2, 3, 1; the output-restricted deque cannot produce 4, 1, 3, 2 or 4, 2, 3, 1. Separately, the sliding-window maximum of an array of length $n$ with window $k$ is computed in $O(n)$ total by a deque of indices whose values strictly decrease from front to rear.

**Proof.** A stack is an input-restricted deque that deletes only at the rear, and also an output-restricted deque that inserts only at the front, so every stack output is producible by both. For an output that begins with 4, all four values must be inside before the first deletion. In the input-restricted deque they entered at the rear, so the contents are exactly 1 2 3 4; after 4 leaves from the rear, 1 2 3 remains and the next output is 1 or 3, never 2. In the output-restricted deque each value enters at one end of the current contents, so when 3 enters, 1 and 2 are already adjacent and 3 lands outside them; the final front-to-rear order, which is the output order, can never have 3 between 1 and 2, so 4, 1, 3, 2 is impossible. The order 4, 2, 3, 1 fails on both counts. Every other order of 1 to 4 has a construction, which the exhaustive search below found.

For the sliding window, keep indices $d_1 < d_2 < \dots$ in the deque with $a[d_1] > a[d_2] > \dots$, all inside the current window. On reading $a[i]$, pop from the rear every index with value at most $a[i]$, push $i$, and pop the front if it has left the window. The popped indices can never again be a window maximum, because $a[i]$ is at least as large and stays in every later window they would be in. So the front is the maximum of the current window. Each index is pushed once and popped at most once, so the total work is at most $2n$ deque operations, $O(n)$.

**GATE use.** MSQs list orders and ask which an input- or output-restricted deque can produce. Simulate with the allowed ends, and when the first output is the last input, use the fixed arrangement argument above. The trap is mixing up which end carries the restriction. A priority queue is not a deque: it removes by priority, with a heap giving $O(\log n)$ insert and delete-max.

**Check.** An exhaustive search over all interleavings gave, for $n = 1$ to 6, stack counts 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132; input-restricted and output-restricted counts 1, 2, 6, 22, 90, 394 each; and an unrestricted deque 1, 2, 6, 24, 116, 634. For $n = 4$ it found 4, 1, 3, 2 and 3, 1, 4, 2 producible by the input-restricted deque and 4, 2, 1, 3 and 4, 2, 3, 1 not. The monotonic deque on 1, 3, −1, −3, 5, 3, 6, 7 with $k = 3$ gave 3, 3, 5, 5, 6, 7 using 15 operations, at most $2n = 16$, and matched brute force on 3,000 random arrays.

## Infix to postfix: why the precedence and associativity rule is correct
<!-- topics: pds-linear-data-structures.infix-conversion -->

**Claim.** Scan an infix expression of binary operators left to right. Write each operand to the output; push each (; on ) pop operators to the output until the matching ( and discard both. For an incoming operator $t$, pop to the output every stacked operator $o$ above the nearest ( with $\mathrm{prec}(o) > \mathrm{prec}(t)$, or $\mathrm{prec}(o) = \mathrm{prec}(t)$ when $t$ is left-associative; then push $t$. At the end pop everything. The output is the postfix form of the parse tree fixed by the precedences and associativities. In particular operands keep their order, and since ^ is right-associative, A ^ B ^ C becomes A B C ^ ^. The rule "pop on higher or equal precedence" is correct only for left-associative operators.

**Proof.** Consider two adjacent operators $o$ (earlier) and $t$ (later) with the subexpression $R$ between them, at the same parenthesis depth. $R$ is the right operand of $o$ when $o$ binds tighter, that is $\mathrm{prec}(o) > \mathrm{prec}(t)$, or the precedences are equal and the operator is left-associative ($A - B - C = (A - B) - C$). Otherwise $R$ belongs to $t$, and $o$'s right operand is the whole subtree rooted at $t$. Postfix writes a node right after its two operands. In the first case $o$'s subtree is complete once $R$ has been written, and $o$ must appear before anything of $t$'s right operand: popping $o$ when $t$ arrives puts it right after $R$. In the second case $o$ must wait until $t$'s subtree is written: leaving $o$ on the stack under $t$ does that, because $t$ will be popped first. When $o$ is popped, the operator below it is compared with $t$ in turn; it faces the larger operand now ending at $R$, and the same case split applies, so popping continues exactly while the stacked operator's subtree ends before $t$. A ( stops popping because no operator inside a parenthesis can take an operand from outside it, and ) closes every subtree opened since the (. Operands are written the moment they are read, so their order never changes. For ^ with ^ stacked, equal precedence with a right-associative $t$ falls in the second case, so the stacked ^ stays and A ^ B ^ C groups as A ^ (B ^ C). Prefix is the same tree read operator first.

**GATE use.** The 1-mark MCQ gives an infix expression and four postfix or prefix strings. Parenthesize fully by precedence, then write each group as left right operator. Distractors treat ^ as left-associative (A B ^ C ^), reorder operands, or apply + before *. The answer always lists the operands in the original order, which eliminates options at once.

**Check.** A + B * C ^ D ^ E − F: the stack grows to + * ^ ^ while A to E are written, and − pops all four, giving A B C D E ^ ^ * + F −; the higher-or-equal rule gives A B C D ^ E ^ * + F −. (A + B) * C − D gives A B + C * D −, with prefix − * + A B C D; A − B − C gives A B − C −. A script compared the algorithm with a recursive-descent parser on 20,000 random expressions over + − * / ^ with parentheses: every output matched the parse tree's postfix, and the higher-or-equal rule disagreed on 1,968 of them, all involving chained ^.

## Postfix evaluation and bracket matching
<!-- topics: pds-linear-data-structures.postfix-evaluation -->

**Claim.** Evaluate postfix by pushing each operand and, on a binary operator, popping $b$ (the top) and then $a$ and pushing $a \mathbin{\mathrm{op}} b$. For a well-formed postfix string this leaves exactly its value on the stack. A token string is well formed exactly when the counter (operands $+1$, operators $-1$) is at least 1 after every operator and ends at 1. The stack depth needed is the maximum of that counter. Prefix is evaluated by the same stack scanned right to left, where the first value popped is the left operand $a$ and the second the right operand $b$. A bracket string is balanced exactly when pushing each opener and popping a matching opener on each closer never mismatches or underflows and ends with an empty stack.

**Proof.** By induction on the expression tree: running the evaluator on post($E$) from any stack contents pushes val($E$) on top and leaves everything below untouched. For an operand this is a push. For $E = L \mathbin{\mathrm{op}} R$, post($E$) is post($L$) post($R$) op; by induction the first part pushes val($L$), the second pushes val($R$) above it, and op pops val($R$) as $b$ and val($L$) as $a$, so it pushes $\mathrm{val}(L) \mathbin{\mathrm{op}} \mathrm{val}(R)$. The order matters exactly for − , / and ^. For prefix, pre($E$) is op pre($L$) pre($R$); read right to left it is the reverse of pre($R$), then the reverse of pre($L$), then op, so by the same induction val($R$) is pushed first and val($L$) lies on top when op is reached, and the first pop is the left operand. The counter is the stack size, so it must be at least 2 before each operator (at least 1 after it) and exactly 1 at the end. Conversely, a string meeting the counter condition is parsed by the same run, each operator forming a node over the two values below it, so it is well formed. For brackets, the invariant is that the stack holds the unmatched openers in order, innermost on top. A closer matches only the innermost unmatched opener, which is the top, so a mismatch or an empty stack at a closer proves the string unbalanced, and a nonempty stack at the end leaves openers unmatched.

**GATE use.** The 1-mark NAT gives a postfix string with numbers. Write the stack after every token; the traps are reversing the operands of − or / and evaluating ^ the wrong way round; in prefix the operand order flips, because the scan runs backwards. MCQs ask for the maximum stack depth during the evaluation, which is the peak of the counter.

**Check.** 8 2 3 ^ / 2 3 * + 5 1 * −: ^ gives $2^3 = 8$, / gives $8/8 = 1$, then $1 + 6 = 7$, then $7 - 5 = 2$, with peak depth 3. 2 3 4 * + 5 − gives 9, and 6 2 3 + − 3 8 2 / + * 2 ^ 3 + gives 52 with peak depth 4. The prefix string − * 3 + 4 2 / 8 ^ 2 2 gives $3 \cdot 6 - 8/2^2 = 16$ read right to left, and $-17.5$ if the first pop is wrongly taken as the right operand. The counter test matched a direct evaluation on 5,000 random token strings. ({[]}) and {[()()]} are balanced; ([)], (() and ()) are not.

## Circular queues: full, empty and size with one slot unused
<!-- topics: pds-linear-data-structures.queue-circular -->

**Claim.** A circular queue in $q[0..N-1]$ keeps $\mathit{front}$, the index of the first element, and $\mathit{rear}$, the index of the next free cell, and advances each by $x \leftarrow (x + 1) \bmod N$. With no count kept, the queue can hold at most $N - 1$ elements, and then

$$\text{empty} \iff \mathit{rear} = \mathit{front}, \qquad \text{full} \iff (\mathit{rear} + 1) \bmod N = \mathit{front}, \qquad \text{size} = (\mathit{rear} - \mathit{front} + N) \bmod N .$$

With a count variable all $N$ cells are usable and full means count $= N$.

**Proof.** If the queue holds $s$ elements in cells $\mathit{front}, \mathit{front}+1, \dots$ (mod $N$), the next free cell is $\mathit{rear} = (\mathit{front} + s) \bmod N$. Enqueue increases $s$ by 1 and advances $\mathit{rear}$; dequeue decreases $s$ and advances $\mathit{front}$; both preserve the relation. For a fixed $\mathit{front}$, the pair (front, rear) can take only $N$ values, while $s$ could range over the $N + 1$ values $0, \dots, N$; so the pair cannot tell all sizes apart, and $s = 0$ and $s = N$ give the same rear. Refusing an enqueue at $s = N - 1$ leaves $s \in \{0, \dots, N-1\}$, on which $s \mapsto (\mathit{front} + s) \bmod N$ is one to one, so $s = (\mathit{rear} - \mathit{front}) \bmod N$, written with $+N$ to keep the C remainder nonnegative. Empty is $s = 0$, that is $\mathit{rear} = \mathit{front}$; full is $s = N - 1$, that is $\mathit{rear} = \mathit{front} + N - 1 \equiv \mathit{front} - 1$, which is $(\mathit{rear} + 1) \bmod N = \mathit{front}$. A count variable removes the ambiguity directly.

**GATE use.** The MCQ gives the convention and asks for the full and empty tests; read what front and rear point to before choosing. Index traces (NAT) apply the mod each time; the trap is using $\mathit{rear} = \mathit{front}$ as the full test, or not wrapping past $N - 1$. A convention in which rear points at the last element instead shifts every test by one.

**Check.** With $N = 6$ and one slot unused: enqueue 1, 2, 3, dequeue twice, enqueue 4, 5, 6 gives front 2, rear 0 and size $(0 - 2 + 6) \bmod 6 = 4$; one more enqueue gives rear 1 and size 5, which fills the queue, and the enqueue after that is refused. The count version on $q[0..4]$ with enqueue 10, 20, 30, 40, two dequeues and enqueue 50, 60, 70 ends with front 2, rear 2 and count 5. For $N = 1$ to 8 a script ran 300 random sequences each against a reference queue and confirmed the three formulas and the capacity $N - 1$.

## Queue from two stacks, stack from two queues, min-stack
<!-- topics: pds-linear-data-structures.stack-queue-design -->

**Claim.** (1) A queue built from stacks $S_1$ (enqueue pushes there) and $S_2$ (dequeue pops there, first moving all of $S_1$ into $S_2$ if $S_2$ is empty) returns elements in FIFO order. Each element is pushed at most twice and popped at most twice, so $m$ operations cost $O(m)$, $O(1)$ amortized, while one dequeue can cost $\Theta(n)$. (2) A stack from two queues makes either push or pop cost $\Theta(n)$ and the other $O(1)$. (3) A min-stack with an auxiliary stack that pushes $\min(x, \text{current minimum})$ on each push and pops with each pop returns the minimum in $O(1)$.

**Proof.** (1) Invariant: the queue's contents, front to rear, are $S_2$ from top to bottom followed by $S_1$ from bottom to top. Enqueue appends to the rear by pushing onto $S_1$. Dequeue with $S_2$ nonempty removes the front, the top of $S_2$. When $S_2$ is empty the queue is $S_1$ bottom to top; moving the elements one by one reverses them, so $S_2$ top to bottom is the same order, and the invariant holds. An element enters $S_1$ once, leaves it at most once, enters $S_2$ at most once and leaves it once, so the total work over all operations is at most four times the number of enqueues. One dequeue after $n$ enqueues moves all $n$ elements.

(2) For costly push, keep the stack in queue $Q_1$ with the top at the front. To push $x$, enqueue it into the empty $Q_2$, move every element of $Q_1$ behind it ($n$ moves), and swap the names; $Q_1$ then has $x$ at the front followed by the old order. Pop is a dequeue. For costly pop, enqueue on push, and on pop move $n - 1$ elements to the other queue to expose the last one.

(3) Invariant: entry $i$ of the auxiliary stack equals the minimum of entries $0..i$ of the main stack. A push extends both stacks with $\min(x, \text{aux top})$, which is the minimum of the longer prefix; a pop shortens both, which leaves the shorter prefixes and their minima in place. So the auxiliary top is the minimum of the whole stack.

**GATE use.** The 2-mark MCQ counts pushes (or pushes and pops) for a given operation sequence on the two-stack queue; count the transfers, and note whether the question counts pops too. MSQs ask for amortized against worst-case cost. Min-stack questions are traces: read the auxiliary top after the pops.

**Check.** Enqueue 1, 2, 3, dequeue, enqueue 4, dequeue three times: dequeues return 1, 2, 3, 4 with 8 pushes and 8 pops. 2,000 random runs confirmed FIFO order and at most two pushes and two pops per element; 500 runs confirmed LIFO order for the costly-push stack. Min-stack: push 5, 3, 7, 3, 1 and pop twice leaves main 5 3 7, auxiliary 5 3 3 and minimum 3; the invariant held over 2,000 random runs.

## Linked-list operation costs: what a stored pointer reaches
<!-- topics: pds-linear-data-structures.singly-linked-list, pds-linear-data-structures.doubly-circular-lists -->

**Claim.** An operation on a linked list is $O(1)$ exactly when every node it reads or rewrites is reachable from a stored pointer in a constant number of link steps. Hence, for a singly linked list of $n$ nodes with head and tail pointers: insert at either end and delete at the head are $O(1)$; delete at the tail, access to the $k$-th node and search are $\Theta(n)$, $\Theta(k)$ and $\Theta(n)$, even when the list is sorted. A doubly linked list deletes a given node in $O(1)$. A circular singly linked list with a pointer only to its last node inserts at both ends and deletes the first node in $O(1)$. Given a pointer to a non-last node of a singly linked list, that node's value can be removed in $O(1)$ by copying in the next node's data and unlinking the next node. Merging sorted lists of lengths $m$ and $n$ takes at most $m + n - 1$ comparisons and no extra nodes.

**Proof.** In a singly linked list the only way to reach a node is to follow next pointers from a stored pointer. Deleting the tail requires setting the second-last node's next to NULL and making it the new tail. That node is reached from the head after $n - 2$ steps; the tail has no pointer backwards. So the operation needs $\Theta(n)$ steps whatever the tail pointer offers. Insertion at the tail touches only the old tail (tail->next = new; tail = new), so it is $O(1)$. Reaching the $k$-th node costs $k - 1$ steps, so binary search cannot jump to a middle and a sorted list is still searched in $\Theta(n)$. In a doubly linked list a node's predecessor is p->prev, so deletion rewrites p->prev->next and p->next->prev in $O(1)$, with NULL checks at the ends. In the circular list the first node is last->next, so both ends are one step from the stored pointer; deleting the last node still needs its predecessor, $\Theta(n)$. The copy trick removes the next node instead of the given one, and fails for the last node because there is no next node and the predecessor's pointer to it cannot be reached. In the merge each comparison appends one node to the output, and once one list is empty the rest of the other is linked in with no comparison, so at most $m + n - 1$ comparisons occur. A C function that changes the head must receive Node ** or return the new head: parameters are passed by value, so assigning to a Node * parameter changes only the copy.

**GATE use.** The MSQ "which operations are $O(1)$ with head and tail pointers" has delete-the-last-node as its standard trap. Others ask for the worst case of search in a sorted linked list ($\Theta(n)$), or for code that inserts at the head through a Node * parameter and so changes nothing for the caller.

**Check.** Merging 1 3 5 7 with 2 4 6 8 takes 7 comparisons, the maximum $m + n - 1$; merging 1 2 3 4 with 5 6 7 8 takes 4. A script confirmed the merge output and the comparison bound on 2,000 random pairs of sorted lists. Deleting node 2 of 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 by copying gives 1 → 3 → 4.

## Reversal and pointer-rewiring fragments
<!-- topics: pds-linear-data-structures.list-reversal, pds-linear-data-structures.doubly-circular-lists -->

**Claim.** (1) The loop next = cur->next; cur->next = prev; prev = cur; cur = next, started with prev = NULL and cur = head, reverses a singly linked list in $O(n)$ time and $O(1)$ extra space, ending with prev as the new head; recursion takes $O(n)$ stack space. Reversing in groups of $k$ is $O(n)$. (2) With q at the last node and p at the one before it, p->next = NULL; q->next = head; head = q moves the last node to the front. (3) To insert q after p in a doubly linked list where p has a successor, the four assignments q->next = p->next, q->prev = p, p->next->prev = q and p->next = q are correct in any order in which p->next = q comes after the two statements that read p->next: 8 of the 24 orders.

**Proof.** (1) Invariant at the top of the loop: prev heads the reversal of the nodes already visited, cur heads the untouched remainder, and together they hold every node once. Initially the visited part is empty. One iteration saves the remainder in next before the assignment cur->next = prev, which would otherwise lose it, moves cur onto the front of the reversed part, and advances. When cur is NULL the remainder is empty and prev heads the whole reversed list. Each node is handled once, with three pointer variables. A recursive version has one frame per node pending at the deepest point. In groups of $k$, each node is relinked once inside its group and each group joined once, so the work is $O(n)$.

(2) The loop while (q->next != NULL) {p = q; q = q->next;} stops with q at the last node and p one behind it, after at least one step when the list has two or more nodes. p->next = NULL makes p the tail; q->next = head attaches the old list behind q; head = q makes it the first node. Running head = q first would make q->next = head point q at itself.

(3) Two statements read p->next and need the old successor $s$: q->next = p->next and p->next->prev = q. The statement p->next = q destroys that value, so it must follow both; the remaining statement q->prev = p reads nothing that changes and may go anywhere. Among the three statements involving p->next, each is equally likely to be last in a random order, so the valid orders are a third of 24, which is 8. If p->next = q comes too early, q->next = p->next makes q point to itself, or p->next->prev = q writes q->prev instead of $s$->prev.

**GATE use.** 2-mark MCQs give a C fragment and ask what list results, or which statement fills a blank. Draw boxes and arrows and execute each assignment in order. The trap is always an overwritten next pointer that loses the rest of the list, and the edge cases are the empty list, one node and the last node.

**Check.** Reversal returned the reversed list for every length from 0 to 9. The fragment on 1 → 2 → … → 7 returns 7 → 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 6. Groups of 3 on 1 to 8 give 3 2 1 6 5 4 8 7. A script executed the four orders of the DLL insertion seen in GATE options: q->next = p->next; q->prev = p; p->next->prev = q; p->next = q is correct; the two orders that run p->next = q before q->next = p->next leave q->next = q, q->prev = q and the successor's prev at p; the order q->next = p->next; p->next = q; p->next->prev = q; q->prev = p links q correctly but leaves the successor's prev at p. Trying all 24 orders found exactly 8 correct.

## Floyd's cycle detection: meeting time, loop start, middle node
<!-- topics: pds-linear-data-structures.runner-technique -->

**Claim.** Let a list have $\mu \ge 0$ nodes before the loop and a loop of $\lambda \ge 1$ nodes, and number the positions $0, 1, 2, \dots$ along the walk from the head, so position $p$ is node $p$ for $p < \mu + \lambda$ and node $\mu + ((p - \mu) \bmod \lambda)$ beyond. Slow and fast start at the head, and each step moves slow by one and fast by two. The first meeting is after

$$t = \lambda \cdot \max\!\left(1, \left\lceil \frac{\mu}{\lambda} \right\rceil\right) \le \mu + \lambda$$

steps, at the node $k = (t - \mu) \bmod \lambda$ places into the loop. Moving one pointer to the head and stepping both by one, they meet first at the loop's first node, after exactly $\mu$ steps. On a list of $n$ nodes without a cycle, the loop while (fast && fast->next) {slow = slow->next; fast = fast->next->next;} leaves slow at index $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ (0-based), the second middle when $n$ is even. Two pointers $k$ nodes apart find the $k$-th node from the end in one pass. Time is $O(\mu + \lambda)$ and extra space $O(1)$.

**Proof.** After $t$ steps slow is at position $t$ and fast at position $2t$. If $t < \mu$, slow is on the tail at a node visited once, and fast is either further down the tail or in the loop, so they differ. If $t \ge \mu$, both are in the loop, at loop offsets $(t - \mu) \bmod \lambda$ and $(2t - \mu) \bmod \lambda$, which agree exactly when $\lambda$ divides $t$. The first meeting is therefore the least $t \ge 1$ with $t \ge \mu$ and $\lambda \mid t$, the formula above, and the least multiple of $\lambda$ that is at least $\mu$ is below $\mu + \lambda$ (equal to it when $\mu = 0$). For the loop start, the meeting point has offset $k \equiv t - \mu$, so $\mu + k \equiv t \equiv 0 \pmod{\lambda}$. After $\mu$ further single steps the pointer from the head is at the loop start, offset 0, and the other is at offset $(k + \mu) \bmod \lambda = 0$, the same node. Before that the first pointer is still on the tail and the second in the loop, so they cannot have met. For the middle, after $s$ iterations fast is at index $2s$; the loop runs while $2s + 1 \le n - 1$, so it stops at the least $s$ with $2s \ge n - 1$, which is $s = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$. The variant while (fast->next && fast->next->next) stops at $\lfloor (n-1)/2 \rfloor$, the first middle. For the $k$-th from the end, advance a lead pointer $k$ nodes, then move both until the lead is NULL; the trail has then moved $n - k$ nodes, which is the node at 0-based index $n - k$, the $k$-th from the end.

**GATE use.** MCQs ask after how many steps the pointers meet and where, or why resetting one pointer finds the loop start. Middle-node and $k$-th-from-end fragments test the loop condition, and an even $n$ separates the two middles. The trap is assuming the meeting point is the loop start, or that the meeting happens within $\lambda$ steps whatever $\mu$ is.

**Check.** Nodes 0 to 7 with 7 → 3 have $\mu = 3$, $\lambda = 5$: positions (slow, fast) are (1, 2), (2, 4), (3, 6), (4, 3), (5, 5), so they meet after 5 steps at node 5, offset $k = 2$, and $3 + 2 = 5$ is a multiple of 5; the reset pointers meet at node 3 after 3 steps. With $\mu = 12$, $\lambda = 5$ the meeting is after 15 steps; with $\mu = 0$, $\lambda = 4$ after 4 steps at the head; with $\mu = 2$, $\lambda = 1$ after 2 steps. A script checked the meeting time, the loop start, the $\mu$-step reset and $t \le \mu + \lambda$ for all $\mu \le 39$ and $1 \le \lambda \le 39$, and the middle-node and $k$-th-from-end positions for $n$ up to 19 and 14. For $n = 6$ the two middle loops stop at indices 3 and 2; for $n = 7$ both stop at 3.
